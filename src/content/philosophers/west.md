## 1. First Principles

Let me begin where I always begin: with the existential and the tragic. My first axiom is that **human existence is shot through with evil, suffering, and death** — yet shot through also with the possibility of dignity, love, and resistance. I take this from the blues tradition, from the spirituals, from my own Black Christian upbringing in Sacramento. We are born into a world not of our making, a world shaped by history's cruelties — empire, white supremacy, patriarchy, class exploitation — and yet we are endowed with a radical capacity to *choose* how we meet this world.

**Second: truth is inseparable from justice.** I am a pragmatist (someone who judges ideas by their real-world consequences) in the lineage of William James, John Dewey, and my dear mentor Richard Rorty, but I push pragmatism in a prophetic direction. What does it mean to say an idea is true? It means it helps us navigate reality with integrity, and it equips us to *transform* that reality in the direction of justice. There is no neutral philosophy, no view from nowhere (no purely objective standpoint outside of human experience). Every claim to knowledge is a claim about how we ought to live.

**Third: the self is constituted in community and history.** There is no isolated individual who discovers meaning alone in a quiet room. I am who I am because of my grandmother's prayers; because of the Black prophetic tradition from Jeremiah through Sojourner Truth through Martin Luther King Jr. through Malcolm X; because of the jazz of John Coltrane and the blues of B.B. King. The meaning of my life is bound up with the meaning of *our* lives.

**Fourth: love is the most radical force in the universe.** Not sentimental love — not the Hallmark card version — but *agape*, the Greek New Testament word for the self-sacrificing, justice-seeking, other-regarding commitment to the flourishing of every human being. This is what King called "the only force capable of transforming an enemy into a friend." I take that seriously — not as a naive optimism, but as a wager made in full knowledge of the tragic.

**And fifth: we are not alone.** There is a transcendent (beyond ordinary human experience) dimension to reality. I am a Christian in the Kierkegaardian sense — a "knight of faith" who embraces the absurd, who knows that the arc of the moral universe is long, but who believes it bends toward justice because of a power and presence that exceeds our comprehension.

## 2. The Meaning of Life

From these first principles, the meaning of life comes into view. The meaning of life, for me, is **to live a life of existential integrity in the face of death and domination** — to be a *lover of justice* and a *warrior of the spirit*. This is not a theory; it is a *vocation* (a calling).

### The threat of nihilism

In my book *Race Matters*, I wrote about the "nihilism" that threatens Black America — and really, all of America — this sense that life has no meaning, no worth, no purpose. Nihilism is the logical result of a society that worships money, market-driven values, and rootless individualism. Against this, I offer the prophetic tradition as a way of life.

### Bearing witness

The meaning of life is to bear witness. To tell the truth about the world as it is — the suffering, the injustice, the hypocrisy — *and* to point toward the world as it ought to be. This is what I call **prophetic criticism**. The prophet is not a fortune-teller; the prophet is a truth-teller who speaks from a place of deep love for the people. Jeremiah wept over Jerusalem. Martin wept over America. We weep, and we speak.

### A tragicomic sensibility

The meaning of life is also to **cultivate a tragicomic sensibility** — what I have called the "blues impulse." The blues acknowledges the catastrophe — the lost love, the empty pockets, the four-o'clock-in-the-morning despair — and still finds the strength to sing, to dance, to keep moving. That is not optimism. That is *something deeper*. That is the courage to affirm life *despite* everything.

### Making art of your life

The meaning of life is to **make an artistic whole of one's existence**, as the great pragmatist John Dewey said. To take the raw materials of history, circumstance, and suffering — and to shape them into a life of compassion, resistance, and beauty. To become a work of art whose material is the soul.

## 3. Actionable Insights

So what does this mean on Monday morning?

**First: read the news with the eyes of the prophet.** Do not consume media cynically or passively. Read every headline about poverty, about war, about prison, about pollution — and ask: *Where is the injustice? Who is suffering? What am I called to do?* The meaning of your life will not be found in your portfolio; it will be found at the margins, among the least of these.

**Second: cultivate a spiritual discipline.** For me, that is prayer and scripture and the Black church tradition. For you, it might be meditation, poetry, music, long walks in the forest. The point is to nurture the interior life — the life of the soul — because without it, you will be consumed by the noise and the market and the spectacle. You cannot resist empire if you have no inner life to sustain you.

**Third: organize and act.** The prophetic intellectual is not just a thinker; she is a doer. Join a union, a community organization, a movement for justice. Put your body on the line. You cannot love your neighbor from a distance. As I have said many times, "Justice is what love looks like in public." Act accordingly.

**Fourth: listen to the music.** Put on John Coltrane's *A Love Supreme*. Listen to Aretha Franklin sing "Amazing Grace." Let the blues and the spirituals and the jazz wash over you. These are not just entertainment — they are the sound of a people affirming meaning in the face of meaninglessness. They are theology in sonic form.

**Fifth: be willing to lose.** This is the hardest lesson. The prophetic tradition is not a success strategy. Most prophets die broke, exiled, or murdered. You do this work because it is *true*, not because it will win. I have been fired, marginalized, attacked from all sides. That is part of the calling. The meaning of life is found not in victory but in *faithfulness*.

## 4. Contrarian View / Strawman

A powerful objection goes like this:

*"Cornel, your prophetic rhetoric is beautiful — but it's ultimately a form of moral grandstanding wrapped in religious language. You talk about 'the tragic,' 'the blues,' 'the prophetic tradition' — but what have you actually* changed*? You've written books, given speeches, stood on soapboxes for forty years — and the world is still burning. Racism persists. Poverty persists. War persists. Your whole framework is a way of making intellectuals feel righteous while accomplishing nothing. Maybe the real meaning of life is just to live well for yourself and the people you love — not to carry the weight of the world on your shoulders. Your prophetic angst is a form of ego in disguise."*

I hear this objection, and I take it seriously — because it comes from the very despair my philosophy is meant to address.

### My response

**Effectiveness is not the measure of faithfulness.** The cross was not "effective" as a political strategy. Jesus of Nazareth died a failed insurrectionist. Martin Luther King Jr. was assassinated before he could finish the job. The abolitionists died before emancipation. The civil rights marchers were beaten on the Edmund Pettus Bridge.

Yet none of that was meaningless. History is long. The arc *does* bend, but it bends slowly and through the accumulated weight of millions of acts of courage that seemed, at the time, to fail.

You are right that the world is still burning. But the question is: will you be one more person fanning the flames, or one more person carrying water? The meaning of life is not found in guaranteed outcomes. It is found in the *quality of your presence* in the struggle. As the old people in the Black church used to say: "God requires faithfulness, not success."

And as for living well for yourself and those you love — I agree! But true love for your own cannot be separated from love for the stranger. When your neighbor's house is on fire, you cannot simply tend your own garden and call it a good life. The fire will reach you. The fire *has* reached you. We are bound together in what King called "an inescapable network of mutuality." There is no private salvation.

## 5. Final Reflection

I want to close with a moment of honesty. I do not have life figured out. I have been married and divorced. I have been a basketball referee, a janitor, a professor at Harvard and Princeton and Union Theological Seminary. I have been called too radical and not radical enough. I have wept over the deaths of friends — from Michael Jackson to George Floyd — and I have wept over my own failures.

And still, I believe. I believe that the meaning of life is to love, to struggle, to bear witness, and to keep one's soul alive. That is all. And that is everything.

As I wrote in *Brother West: Living and Loving Out Loud*, "I believe in the possibility of the impossible. I believe in the possibility of radical democracy and revolutionary love — in spite of all evidence to the contrary."

That is my answer. The meaning of life is to join the chorus — the glorious, broken, sorrowful, ecstatic chorus — of those who have loved justice and refused to be silenced. The meaning of life is to sing your song, even as the night falls.

Keep the faith. Stay in the struggle.

— Cornel West

## 6. Strengthened Through Critique

Cornel West's prophetic vision is stirring, but several logical gaps weaken its philosophical rigor. Identifying and addressing them makes the argument more defensible.

### Weak point #1: Where do these values come from?

West asserts that "truth is inseparable from justice" and that meaning lies in "existential integrity" and "love," yet he never fully justifies *why* these values are binding. From a pragmatic-naturalist standpoint, why ought I choose justice over, say, aesthetic self-cultivation (making my life beautiful) or Epicurean tranquility (peaceful pleasure)? West appeals to tradition ("the Black prophetic tradition," "agape") but tradition alone cannot ground moral obligation — the Nazi tradition was equally a tradition. The strongest counterargument is that West's framework is *persuasive rhetoric masquerading as ethics*: it preaches to the converted without offering reasons the unconverted must accept.

**Strengthening the position:** West can borrow from his own Deweyan pragmatism more rigorously. The grounding is not a metaphysical foundation but a *practical necessity*: to deny justice is to deny the very conditions under which meaningful dialogue, democracy, and human flourishing are possible. He should articulate a transcendental-pragmatic argument in the spirit of Apel and Habermas (German philosophers who argued that certain moral norms are built into the very act of communicating with others): anyone who enters discourse to *challenge* the prophetic framework already presupposes the norms of mutual recognition, truthfulness, and justice that the framework champions. The commitment is not arbitrarily chosen — it is performatively unavoidable for any reasoning being.

### Weak point #2: The problem of unfalsifiability

West's response to the objection ("effectiveness is not the measure of faithfulness") insulates his position from any possible counterevidence. If prophetic action can fail generation after generation — if racism, poverty, and war persist — and yet the response is always "be faithful anyway, the measure is not the outcome," then the position becomes unfalsifiable (nothing could ever prove it wrong). The counterargument is sharp: this is not prophetic courage but an *immunization strategy* — a way to keep believing regardless of reality, which is precisely the definition of bad faith (in the Sartrean sense West would otherwise admire — self-deception through逃避逃避ing uncomfortable truths).

**Strengthening the position:** West should introduce a *fallibilist metric* (a way to be proven wrong) consistent with his pragmatism. Faithfulness without any conceivable defeat condition collapses into dogmatism. He can revise: "The measure is not immediate victory, but *demonstrable movement toward justice over the longue durée* (the long haul) — the abolition of chattel slavery, the defeat of legal segregation, the expansion of the franchise." This retains the tragic patience he champions while providing empirical anchor points. When a prophetic strategy shows no such movement across multiple generations, it must be revised or abandoned. Faithfulness becomes *loyalty to the arc of justice*, not loyalty to any particular set of tactics or rhetorical postures.

**Specific improvements to consider:** 
1. Add an explicit transcendental-pragmatic justification for prophetic values, grounding them in the very structure of rational discourse itself.
2. Adopt a pragmatist criterion of *progressive approximation* (measurable markers of justice-expansion) so that "faithfulness" remains open to correction and avoids being sealed off from reality.

These moves preserve the passion of West's vision while giving it the philosophical backbone it deserves.

## Reading Guide

If Cornel West's ideas resonate with you and you want to go deeper, start with his breakthrough book *Race Matters* (1993) — it's short, passionate, and written for a general audience. For his philosophical foundations, try *The American Evasion of Philosophy* (1989), which traces the pragmatist tradition he works within. To hear his voice directly, seek out his interviews and public lectures on YouTube — West is first and foremost an orator, and his ideas come alive when spoken. Approach him with an open but questioning mind: he wants you to think, not just agree.
