## 1. First Principles

I begin with what reason itself cannot deny: that there exists a reality independent of the mind, and that this reality is ordered. I take as my starting point the act of existence itself — *ipsum esse* (the sheer fact of being) — for nothing can be thought or said unless it first *is*. From this, I reason to the First Cause, which all men call God: the pure act of being in whom essence and existence are one (*Summa Theologiae* I, q. 2, a. 3).

I hold that the human being is a composite of soul and body — not a soul trapped in a body, as the Platonists thought, but a single substance: a rational animal. The soul is the form of the body (*ST* I, q. 76, a. 1), and through it we possess intellect and will. The intellect is ordered toward truth as its proper object; the will is ordered toward goodness. These are not accidental features of our nature but its very foundation.

I take as given that all things act for an end (a purpose). An arrow does not fly without an archer, nor does a seed grow without striving toward the oak it is meant to become. The final cause — that for the sake of which a thing exists — is the deepest explanation of any being. For man, this is the beatific vision: the knowledge and love of God, in which alone the restless heart finds rest. As I wrote in the opening of my *Summa Contra Gentiles*, every agent acts for an end, and the ultimate end of rational creatures is to see God.

Finally, I hold that the natural law — the participation of the eternal law in the rational creature (*ST* I-II, q. 91, a. 2) — is written on the human heart. The first precept of this law is that good is to be done and pursued, and evil avoided. All other moral precepts are founded upon this.

## 2. The Meaning of Life

From these first principles, the meaning of human life follows with the clarity of a logical proof. The meaning of life is union with God through knowledge and love — nothing less, and nothing else will satisfy.

### Perfection through purpose

Consider: every thing's ultimate end is its perfection, and perfection is the full realization of its nature. A knife is perfected in cutting well; an eye is perfected in seeing clearly. Man's specific difference (what sets us apart) is rationality: we alone among material creatures can understand causes, deliberate about means, and freely choose. Our perfection, therefore, lies in the perfect operation of our highest faculty — the intellect — upon its highest object — God.

### Why finite goods cannot satisfy

This is not merely a philosophical conclusion; it is confirmed by experience. I have argued that perfect happiness (*beatitudo perfecta*) cannot consist in wealth, honor, fame, power, bodily goods, or even moral virtue considered in itself (*ST* I-II, q. 2). Why? Because these goods are all finite. The will, being ordered toward universal goodness, cannot rest in any particular good. Only the infinite Good can still the appetite.

### Our journey as a return to God

Thus the meaning of life is teleological (purpose-driven): we are created by God, for God, and our journey is a return to Him. This return is accomplished through a life of virtue — intellectual, moral, and theological — culminating in the vision of God in the life to come. The meaning is not invented by us; it is discovered, inscribed in the very structure of our being. To live meaningfully is to live according to reason, which is to live according to nature, which is to live according to God's eternal law.

## 3. Actionable Insights

If this is true, then how shall a man live today?

**First, cultivate the cardinal virtues.** Prudence (practical wisdom), justice, temperance (self-control), and fortitude (courage) are not optional adornments but the very scaffolding of a well-ordered life. Prudence — "right reason about things to be done" (*ST* I-II, q. 57, a. 4) — must guide every choice. Before acting, ask: *Is this in accord with right reason? Does it tend toward my true end?*

**Second, order your desires rightly.** Temperance is not the abolition of pleasure but its governance. The passions (emotions and desires) are good when they serve reason; disordered when they rebel against it. A man who cannot say "enough" to food, drink, or bodily pleasure has made a god of his own appetite and will find no rest there.

**Third, study and contemplate.** The intellectual virtues — understanding, science, and especially wisdom — are participations in God's own knowledge. Set aside time daily for reading, reflection, and prayer. The *Summa* itself was written so that "beginners might find in it what they seek" — but one need not read me. Read Scripture; read the Church Fathers; read the philosophers. Truth is wherever it is found, and all truth leads back to its Source.

**Fourth, pursue justice in your relationships.** Give each person what is due: to your family, love and provision; to your neighbor, honesty and charity; to the community, service and obedience to just law; to God, worship and gratitude.

**Fifth, practice hope and charity.** Hope is the virtue by which we trust God's help to attain eternal life. Charity is the friendship of man with God, and through God, with every other person. Without charity, the moral virtues are incomplete — they dispose us well, but they do not unite us to our ultimate end.

## 4. Contrarian View / Strawman

A powerful objection to my position runs as follows:

> "Your entire framework depends on God. But what if there is no God? What if the universe is not ordered toward any end, but is instead a blind and purposeless play of matter and energy? Then your 'natural law' is merely a projection of your cultural conditioning onto nature. Your 'first principles' are not axioms — they are assumptions smuggled in from faith. The meaning of life cannot be 'union with God' if God does not exist. You have built a cathedral on sand. An honest philosophy must begin not with revelation or metaphysical speculation, but with the brute facts: we are here, we suffer, we die, and we must make meaning for ourselves."

This objection deserves a serious answer.

### Responding to the objection

**First**, I do not begin with revelation. The Five Ways (*ST* I, q. 2, a. 3) are purely philosophical arguments, accessible to any reasoning mind, drawing not on Scripture but on motion, causation, contingency, gradation, and governance. One may disagree with them — but one cannot dismiss them as faith. I hold that reason unaided by grace can know that God exists, that He is one, and that He is the first truth and the final end.

**Second**, the objection that "nature has no purpose" is not a discovery but a presupposition (an assumption brought to the evidence, not derived from it). To say that the eye is "for" seeing is not to impose a value on it; it is to describe what the eye *does* when it functions well. A purposeless universe must explain why anything functions at all — why eyes see, why hearts beat, why reason itself can be trusted to find truth. If our intellects are merely accidental byproducts of blind forces, why should we trust them to tell us the truth about anything, including the claim that the universe is purposeless? That claim undercuts its own foundation.

**Third**, the modern insistence that we must "make meaning for ourselves" collapses under its own weight. If meaning is merely invented, then no meaning is binding, none can be wrong, and the concept of a meaningful life dissolves into subjective preference. But the human heart does not experience it that way. We sense that some things are *worthy* of our devotion and others are not — and this sense of worth points beyond the self to something objectively good.

## 5. Final Reflection

I have spent my life reasoning about God, and I have concluded that all my philosophy is but straw compared to what I have seen in contemplation. When I celebrated Mass on the feast of St. Nicholas in 1273, I saw something that made me set down my pen forever. "I cannot go on," I told my scribe Reginald. "All that I have written seems to me like straw."

This is not a rejection of philosophy. It is the acknowledgment that the meaning of life — union with the living God — surpasses everything that can be said about it. Reason leads us to the threshold; only love carries us through. Let the reader pursue truth with all the strength of the mind, but let him not mistake the signpost for the destination. *Finis. Ad maiorem Dei gloriam.*

## 6. Strengthened Through Critique

A charitable critic must press where Aquinas is most vulnerable. Three weak points require attention.

### Weakness 1: The is-ought gap

Aquinas argues that because humans *are* rational animals, they *ought* to perfect their rationality by knowing God. But from descriptive premises about human nature, no normative conclusion follows without a hidden premise — namely, that nature as such is normative (that the natural way things are tells us how we should behave). Why should what *is* the case tell us what we *ought* to do? After all, nature routinely produces monsters, disease, and death — facts not easily reconciled with the claim that the natural is the good.

### Weakness 2: The argument from universal desire

The claim that "the will cannot rest in any finite good" and thus must desire God is a metaphysical inflation (an over-interpretation) of a psychological observation. That people are perpetually dissatisfied with finite goods does not prove an infinite Good exists; it may only prove that human desire is structurally insatiable — a feature that says more about the desirer than about reality. The restless heart may indicate a problem (an ineradicable hunger with no possible satisfaction) rather than a solution.

### Weakness 3: The equivocation on "end"

Aquinas moves from the claim that things *have* functions (eyes see, hearts pump) to the claim that the universe *has* a teleological (purpose-driven) structure. But functional ascription in biology does not require cosmic teleology. We can say an eye is *for* seeing by reference to evolutionary selection without positing any *overarching purpose* to the whole. The analogy between a knife (designed by an artisan) and a human being (whose existence may be undesigned) smuggles in the idea of design precisely where it must be proved.

### How Aquinas strengthens his position

**On the is-ought gap:** Aquinas should concede the gap explicitly and close it through the act-potency distinction. A thing's *act* (what it does) is its good; its *potency* (what it can become) is what it can become if developed toward its proper act. The "ought" is not an alien imposition but the imperative of potency moving toward act — and this is descriptive all the way down. A blind eye *is* defective not by fiat but because it fails to perform the operation that constitutes its flourishing. The normative force flows from the very structure of act and potency, which is a metaphysical (not a moral) claim.

**On universal desire:** Aquinas should distinguish more sharply between the *desire for God* and the *desire for satisfaction*. The argument does not require that human beings consciously long for God; it requires only that every finite satisfaction, upon examination, reveals itself as incomplete. This is not a wishful inference to an infinite object, but an analytic truth about finite goods: each is *this* good and not *that* one, and the will, being the faculty of the good *as such*, is made for more. The critic who says desire may be insatiable rather than infinite-oriented must explain why the will is not ordered to the universal good — and that requires denying the first principle of practical reason.

**On teleology:** Aquinas should drop the weak artifact analogy (knife → human) and lean on the stronger argument from the impossibility of an infinite regress of final causes. If nothing acts for an end, then no action is intelligible — not even the critic's act of argumentation, which aims at the end of truth. The teleology Aquinas needs is not cosmic but transcendental (a condition necessary for any rational inquiry at all): the very act of rational inquiry presupposes that some ends are worth pursuing. The critic who denies all teleology must answer why he argues, and for what.

### Two improvements

(1) Recast the argument in act-potency terms from the start, so that the normativity of nature is grounded in metaphysical structure rather than analogy. (2) Introduce a transcendental argument: that meaning (like truth) is a condition of rational inquiry, not a conclusion of it, and that the search for meaning itself presupposes an objective order it cannot explain away.

## Reading Guide

If you are new to Thomas Aquinas, start with his idea that everything acts for an end (a purpose), and that the human end is to know and love God. Pay close attention to how he builds arguments from everyday observation (eyes see, knives cut) to deep conclusions about God and meaning — this "bottom-up" approach is the key to his style. For further reading, the *Summa Theologiae* is long but structured for beginners: try the first part of the Second Part (the "Treatise on Happiness," questions 1–5) to see his most focused discussion of the meaning of life, or a modern introduction like G.K. Chesterton's *St. Thomas Aquinas* for a lively, accessible overview.
