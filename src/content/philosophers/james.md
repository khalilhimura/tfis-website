## 1. First Principles

**Start with experience, not theory.** I begin with a temper (an attitude) rather than a theorem. The first principle of my philosophy — what I have called *radical empiricism* — is that the only things worth debating among philosophers are things we can define in terms drawn from experience. I take it as given that experience is a continuous, flowing stream, not a mosaic of fixed bits waiting to be assembled by some higher logic. Consciousness is not a *thing* but a *function* — it selects, it chooses, it carves this world of ours out of a much larger, more chaotic "much-at-onceness" that surrounds us on every side.

**The world is still being made.** I hold, further, that the world is still *in the making*. It is not a finished block of being with every relation eternally fixed. Novelty is real. Chance — real chance, not just a name for our ignorance — is a genuine feature of the universe. As I argued in *The Will to Believe*, our passional and volitional nature (our feelings and our will) must play a part in shaping our convictions, especially when the evidence alone is not enough to force a conclusion. We are participants, not spectators. The pragmatist method — the attitude of looking away from first things, principles, "categories," and supposed necessities, and looking instead toward last things, fruits, consequences, and facts — is what I take to be the only honest starting point.

**The self is social.** And finally: the human self is a social self, rooted in many relationships. A man has as many social selves as there are individuals who recognize him. We are, from the outset, embedded in a world of other wills. Truth itself is a species of the good (a kind of goodness) — not a cold match between our ideas and an unchanging reality, but what *works*, what *leads* us forward, what opens up further experience.

## 2. The Meaning of Life (per your philosophy)

**Meaning is not found — it is made.** From these first principles, the meaning of life cannot be a static essence handed down from on high or deduced from a timeless abstraction. Meaning is *forged*. The meaning of life is the *pursuit of the strenuous mood* — the active, earnest engagement with the world's real possibilities, both for good and for ill.

**The moral struggle is where meaning lives.** In *The Varieties of Religious Experience*, I wrote that the visible world is part of a more spiritual universe from which it draws its chief significance. That is the "over-belief" (a belief that goes beyond what can be strictly proven) that many of us — including myself — cannot help but hold. But the meaning is not in gazing backward at that source. It is in the *strenuous moral life* that flows forward from it: the conviction that there is something in the universe worth saving, something that depends on *your* effort, *your* vote, *your* small push against the drift of things.

**Meaning is the difference you make.** Meaning, then, is the *difference you make*. It is found in the moments when the abstract ideal — justice, love, truth — meets the concrete case, and you choose the harder, more demanding path because you sense that the universe is somehow *better* for it. The meaning of life is not a proposition you contemplate; it is a life you live with your whole will, under the pressure of moral demand. As I put it in *Pragmatism*: the true is the name of whatever proves itself to be good in the way of belief. And what proves good is what carries us through the real, messy, unfinished business of living.

If you ask me for a single sentence: *The meaning of life is to take the universe's partial-ness seriously and throw your weight onto the side of its better possibilities.*

## 3. Actionable Insights

What follows? What does a person *do* with this?

### First: Take your impulses seriously.

In my chapter "The Will" in *The Principles of Psychology*, I described how effort of attention is the essential phenomenon of will. You cannot think of a bad thing and push it away by force; you can only attend to something better, something more interesting, something more *vital*. So the first practice is: cultivate the habit of turning your attention toward what is life-giving, promising, and courageous. This is not naive optimism — I detest the "healthy-minded" (those who refuse to look at evil). It is the *strenuous* decision to keep the better possibility in view despite acknowledging the worst.

### Second: Act as if.

Faith in a fact can help create the fact. If you believe a person is trustworthy and treat them as such, you may call forth the very trustworthiness that was not there before. If you doubt the meaning of your own work, do it anyway with full attention, and see what meaning emerges in the doing. The meaning is *in* the action, not behind it.

### Third: Live the experimental life.

Treat your convictions as hypotheses to be tested in the laboratory of experience, not as dogmas to be guarded. Try things. Fail. Revise. The pragmatic temper does not fear error; it fears *stagnation* — the closing off of possibility before the test is run.

### Fourth: Refuse the spectator's posture.

When you are tempted to say "it doesn't matter what I do," remember the moral universe is not a solid block; it is a republic in which your vote counts. Even your small choice — to be kind when you are tired, to speak up when it is risky, to persist when it is wearying — registers in the total.

Concretely, then: wake up each morning and ask, "What is the better possibility that I can help bring about today?" Then do it, knowing full well that failure is real and that no cosmic guarantee covers you. Do it *anyway*.

## 4. Contrarian View / Strawman

A critic — let me give him his full voice — might say:

> "James, you have made a virtue of wishfulness. You say meaning is forged in action and that belief can create its own verification. But this is intellectual suicide. You have abandoned the hard discipline of truth for a warm, subjective glow. What you call 'the will to believe' is really the will to be comforted. You tell a man to believe something because it works *for him* — but what if it works for him to believe he is Napoleon? What if a cruel man's faith in his own righteousness 'works' by your standard? You have no criterion left. You have dissolved truth into utility, and utility into whatever a given temperament finds satisfying. That is not a philosophy of meaning; it is a license for self-deception. And when you speak of 'the strenuous mood' and 'throwing your weight onto better possibilities' — better by what measure? If the universe is genuinely unfinished, as you claim, then there is no fixed 'better' to guide the throw. You are asking a man to leap without a landing. That is not courage; it is recklessness dressed up as virtue."

I hear this objection, and it has force. Let me answer.

**Response: Pragmatism is not just "whatever feels nice."** First: my pragmatism is not a subjectivist swamp (a free-for-all of personal feelings). The "cash-value" of a truth is not whatever feels nice; it is what *works in the long run and on the whole*, taking into account the entire body of experience, including the experience of other people. A belief that I am Napoleon fails — it fails against the brute facts of my life, the testimony of everyone around me, the entire institutional fabric of verification we inherit. What "works" is not a private feeling; it is a public, communal, ongoing process.

**Response: Only for genuine undecidables.** Second: I do not say "believe whatever comforts you." I say that when the evidence is *genuinely undecidable* — and only then — our passional nature (our emotions and will) may rightly tip the scales. This is the domain of *forced, living, momentous* options (choices we must make, that matter deeply, and that we cannot postpone). In matters where action is unavoidable and the intellect alone cannot decide, to refuse to choose because of a fear of error is itself a choice — and often the worse one.

**Response: The risk is the point.** And to the charge that "better" is undefined: I grant it. There is no transcendent guarantee (no outside authority) that tells us, once and for all, what is better. We discover it — or fail to — by living. The meaning of life is not spelled out in advance. That is the risk. That is also the dignity. A scripted drama gives the actor no genuine choice; an improvised one requires courage, attention, and the willingness to be wrong. I choose the improvisation.

## 5. Final Reflection

I am an old man now, writing in the last year of my life from my home in Chocorua. I have spent decades defending the rights of the incomplete, the uncertain, the still-possible. And I confess: I do not know, in any final sense, what the meaning of life is. But I know what it *feels* like to live meaningfully. It feels like the morning after a storm, when you step outside and the air is sharp, and there is work to be done — work that no one else can do for you. It feels like the quiet conviction that your effort matters, even if you cannot prove it. It feels like saying Yes to the universe *despite* the evidence it offers for saying No.

As I wrote in *The Varieties of Religious Experience*, the realistic melancholy of the healthy-minded is a form of blindness. The only complete life is the one that has tasted both the bitter and the sweet, that has known despair *and* found the will to go on.

So go on. The world is waiting for your vote.

— **William James**<br>*Chocorua, New Hampshire, 1910*

## Strengthened Through Critique

**Weak point 1: The criterion of "better" remains procedurally empty.** James tells us to throw our weight onto "better possibilities" and to live "the strenuous moral life," but he concedes in his own voice that there is "no transcendent guarantee" of what counts as better. The pragmatist test — what works "in the long run and on the whole" — is handed down as though it were a method, yet it cannot be applied without already having some criterion of *what counts as working*. Is it survival? Flourishing? Coherence with existing belief? Reduced suffering? Each points in different directions, and the single phrase "in the long run" smuggles in a teleology (a belief in fixed purposes or ends) that James officially rejects. If the universe is genuinely unfinished, then "the long run" never arrives, and the test never settles.

**Strongest counterargument:** This is not a gap in the argument; it is the argument collapsing. A method that cannot specify its own success condition is no method at all — it is a Rorschach blot (an inkblot test) onto which the user projects whatever they already prefer. James's pragmatism, on this reading, does not guide inquiry; it *ratifies* whatever the temperamentally strenuous person was going to do anyway, while offering no resources to adjudicate between, say, the strenuous Nazi and the strenuous abolitionist. Both are throwing their weight onto "better possibilities"; James has no criterion to call one better than the other without appealing to a moral realism his metaphysics denies him.

**How James can strengthen his position:** He can unify his scattered normative commitments into a *procedural criterion of growth in experience*. Drawing on the later pragmatist tradition (Dewey, Addams), James should argue that the "better" is not a fixed end but an *expansion of the range and depth of experience accessible to future inquiry*. A belief is better if it opens up more experience to more people over time — if it connects, enriches, and renders experience more *navigable*. This retains the unfinished universe while supplying a directional test: crushing or foreclosing possibilities counts as failure; expanding and integrating them counts as success. The Nazi's strenuousness fails not because it violates a transcendent moral law, but because it systematically narrows the experiential field for everyone it touches, including himself.

**Weak point 2: The role of truth in "The Will to Believe" is unstable.** James insists we may believe when evidence is undecidable, but he never adequately addresses how we determine *when* evidence is genuinely undecidable. This is not a trivial boundary problem: every fanatic believes the evidence for his cause is decisively clear while the skeptic's evidence is merely ambiguous. James provides no demarcation principle (no clear rule for telling the two cases apart) — only the warning that misuse constitutes "the worst kind of intellectual debauchery," which is an insult where an actual criterion is needed.

**Strongest counterargument:** Without a demarcation principle, James's "will to believe" is a permission slip for motivated irrationality. He cannot have it both ways: either the intellect is competent to judge when evidence is undecidable (in which case the passional nature is superfluous on the very question that matters), or it is not (in which case we have no way to know whether our passionate leap is a legitimate over-belief or a self-serving delusion). The doctrine either dissolves or becomes viciously circular.

**How James can revise:** He should distinguish *two species of undecidability*. The first is *agnostic undecidability* (the evidence is incomplete and no action is forced — we may safely suspend judgment). The second is *practical undecidability* (the evidence is incomplete *and* we must act in a forced, living, momentous option). The will to believe applies *only* to the second species, and its test is not whether the belief feels true but whether acting on it demonstrably *enlarges the field of subsequent experience*. James should frame this as a *regulative principle of risk-taking in inquiry*, not a blanket permission to believe. The question becomes not "May I believe this?" but "Does betting on this hypothesis open up more experience than withholding the bet?"

**Proposed revision to James's central claim:** *The meaning of life is to throw your weight onto possibilities that enlarge the field of shared experience, knowing that the final verdict is never in — and acting as if your effort counts toward a universe worth having, while remaining answerable to the test of experience as it unfolds.* This tightens the criterion without abandoning the unfinished universe, and it places the strenuous life under the discipline of *public and cumulative* inquiry rather than private temperament.

## Reading Guide

If you're new to William James, the best place to start is *The Will to Believe* (a short essay that lays out his core ideas about faith, action, and evidence) or the final chapters of *Pragmatism* (where he makes his case in everyday language). Don't worry about mastering every argument on the first read — James wrote to be felt as much as understood. When you hit a passage that seems contradictory or too loose, sit with it: he often plants a tension on purpose, trusting that living with the question is more important than getting a tidy answer.
