## 1. First Principles

**A biological view of the mind.** Let me start with what I consider the most solid finding of modern cognitive science — one that dissolves the question of life's meaning before we can even properly ask it. Human beings are born with a genetically determined biological endowment that shapes how we learn language, form concepts, and interpret the world. This is not speculation; it is the working conclusion of fifty years of generative grammar (the study of the rules that generate the sentences of a language). The *poverty of the stimulus* argument — the observation that the language a child hears is too incomplete and messy to account for the rich, precise knowledge they end up with — shows that the mind is not a blank slate. It is a highly structured organ, what I have called the *language faculty*, and it limits what counts as a possible human language and, by extension, a possible human thought.

**Why the question misfires.** What follows from this is a severe limitation on what kind of question "the meaning of life" even is. It is not a well-formed scientific question. It does not submit to the kind of empirical investigation that has produced our genuine knowledge. There is no fact of the matter, no discoverable property of the universe, that constitutes a "meaning" waiting to be found. The universe is indifferent. It has no purposes, no intentions, no narrative arc. Consciousness is a biological phenomenon — probably the hardest problem we face, what I have called the "mind-body problem" recast (the ancient puzzle of how physical matter gives rise to subjective experience) — but it is a product of organic evolution, not an emanation from some cosmic order. We are complex organic creatures, nothing more, nothing less.

**Creativity as our core.** So my starting point is this: we are born with a rich inborn structure that makes certain kinds of creativity and inquiry possible; we live in a world without built-in purpose; and we have the capacity — uniquely developed in humans — to exercise what I have called the *creative aspect of language use*, the ability to produce and understand new sentences in ways that are not forced by our internal state or by outside stimulation. That capacity is the core of what it means to be human.

## 2. The Meaning of Life (per your philosophy)

**From theory to action.** If the question has no answer in the way a physicist can give you the mass of an electron, then what is left? What is left is *human action* — choices about how to live, what to value, what to resist. And here my views come not from linguistics but from a different tradition: classical liberalism, anarchist socialism, and the libertarian currents of the Enlightenment.

**Meaning is made, not found.** I take the meaning of human life to be realized through *creative work* and *free association* in a society that maximizes human freedom and minimizes domination. The purpose of life is not to find some external meaning but to *create* it — through intellectual inquiry, through artistic expression, through solidarity with others, and through the unrelenting struggle against illegitimate authority and structures of coercion. This is the thread that runs from Humboldt (the 18th-century philosopher of human development) to Marx to the anarchist tradition I have written about in books like *Problems of Knowledge and Freedom* and *For Reasons of State*.

**Making meaning through action.** More precisely: the meaning of life is to use the innate creative capacities we possess to understand the world, to improve the human condition, and to dismantle the institutions that turn human beings into tools of production or obedience. The Enlightenment idea that all people are equal in their fundamental moral worth — a radical notion, never fully realized — demands that we recognize every person's right to realize their potential free from arbitrary domination. That is not a matter of finding meaning; it is a matter of *making* meaning through action.

**A one-sentence answer.** "The meaning of life," if the phrase must be used at all, is *the relentless pursuit of freedom and the fullest possible development of human creative capacities*. It is not a destination. It is an activity.

## 3. Actionable Insights

**Invest in genuine inquiry.** What follows in practical terms? First: *invest your intellectual energy in genuine inquiry*. Learn a language, study physics, read history, argue with people who disagree with you. The life of the mind is not a luxury; it is the exercise of the faculty that defines our species. The failure of our educational institutions — which I have criticized as designed mainly to train obedient workers and create a docile population, not to cultivate independent thought — is a tragedy precisely because it denies people the tools for this kind of self-realization.

**Organize collectively.** Second: *organize*. The most meaningful actions a person can take are those that reduce human suffering and oppose concentrations of power. I have spent my life doing intellectual work, yes, but also engaging in political activism — from opposing the Vietnam War to supporting the Zapatista uprising in Chiapas (an indigenous rebellion for land and autonomy) to criticizing US-backed atrocities in East Timor and Palestine. Ordinary citizens, when they organize collectively in movements, have changed history: the abolition of slavery, the labor movement, women's suffrage, the civil rights movement, the anti-war movement. None of these were handed down by elites. They were built from the ground up by people who acted on moral conviction.

**Resist commodification.** Third: *resist the commodification of everything* (the process of turning human goods and relationships into market commodities). Modern capitalism has reached the point where almost every aspect of human life — health care, education, housing, even intellectual property — is subordinated to market forces. This is an attack on human freedom. Live simply, consume less, be skeptical of the assumption that more economic growth is the solution to our problems. The climate catastrophe, which will be the defining crisis of this century, is a direct consequence of the relentless drive for profit and the refusal of state-corporate power to consider human survival over short-term gain.

**Recognize your ignorance.** Fourth: *recognize your own ignorance*. One of the most important insights from the study of human cognition is that there are severe limits to what we can understand. The mysteries that science will never solve — and there are many, as I have argued — should humble us. Intellectual humility is not weakness; it is the basis for genuine inquiry.

## 4. Contrarian View / Strawman

**The objection from transcendence.** A powerful objection, and one I take seriously, comes from the religious or existentialist traditions: *You have drained life of all transcendence. You have reduced human existence to a biological accident and called political activism "meaning." But meaning must be objective, must come from somewhere beyond the merely human. If the universe has no purpose, then all your talk of freedom and creativity is just your personal preference, no more valid than anyone else's. The Nazi who finds "meaning" in racial purification is equally justified on your account. You have nothing to ground moral obligation — you have only your own aesthetic preference for anarchism.*

**Why the objection fails.** It is a strong criticism, and I have wrestled with it. My response is this: the objection assumes that an objective source of value — God, the Forms (Plato's eternal ideals), the Moral Law — is necessary for moral obligation to be real. But that assumption is itself unsupported. The roots of moral and political values lie in human nature, in our inborn capacities for empathy, cooperation, and creative expression. The Nazi's "meaning" is *not* equally justified — not because I say so, but because it contradicts the fundamental moral equality of human beings, a principle rooted in our shared humanity and in the requirements of any decent social order. I do not need a divine command to know that causing unnecessary suffering is wrong. It is wrong because it violates the creative, autonomous nature of the beings to whom it is done.

**Living without guarantees.** Furthermore, the demand for transcendent foundations (some ultimate source of value beyond human life) is a demand for certainty that human life cannot provide. We make decisions under conditions of uncertainty. That is what it means to be human. The absence of cosmic guarantees does not excuse indifference — it makes our choices more meaningful, not less. If morality were simply handed down from above, it would require no courage, no thought, no creativity. The very fact that it was *given* would strip it of the human qualities that give it weight.

## 5. Final Reflection

**Why keep going?** I am often asked why I continue to write, speak, and organize at my age, when the problems I have spent a lifetime analyzing seem if anything more deeply entrenched than ever. The answer is simple: there is no alternative. If we accept that human beings have an inborn drive for freedom and creative expression, then to abandon the struggle against oppression is to abandon what makes us human. The meaning of life is not a proposition you can prove. It is a choice you make, every day, about whose side you are on.

**Pessimism with persistence.** I have been called an optimist. I am not. I am a pessimist with a stubborn refusal to stop acting as though a better world is possible. That is the only meaning worth having.

## Strengthened Through Critique

**Weak Point 1: The Is-Ought Gap from Biology to Value**

Chomsky grounds moral obligation in "human nature" — inborn capacities for empathy, cooperation, and creativity — and argues that the Nazi's project is *not* equally justified because it "contradicts the fundamental moral equality of human beings, a principle rooted in our common humanity." But this is a textbook naturalistic fallacy (the mistake of deriving how things *ought to be* from how they *are*): from the fact that humans *are* cooperative and creative, it does not follow that they *ought to be*. Evolution also gave us aggression, tribalism, and cruelty. If biological endowment licenses values, the Nazi can equally point to our inborn xenophobia and territoriality. Chomsky has not shown why *some* inborn traits ground morality while others do not, except by appealing to the very liberal values he is trying to justify.

**Strengthened Response:** Chomsky can drop the appeal to biological foundations for morality and instead ground obligation in a *reflective equilibrium* (the process of balancing general principles against particular judgments) between the inborn creative capacities he privileges and the *performative consistency* of moral argument itself. When the Nazi claims "meaning" in racial purification, he implicitly demands that his own claim be taken seriously — that others recognize his rationality and humanity. Yet the content of his claim denies those very attributes to his victims. This is not a biological contradiction but a *pragmatic* one: the Nazi's position cannot be stated without undermining the conditions of its own utterance. This move — drawing on Habermas's discourse ethics and Kant's universalizability test — gives Chomsky a non-arbitrary basis for rejecting oppressive "meanings" without appealing to transcendent metaphysics.

**Weak Point 2: The Unacknowledged Normative Weight of "Creative Work"**

Chomsky elevates creative work and free association as the *telos* (ultimate purpose) of human life, claiming the purpose is to "create" meaning through inquiry and solidarity. But this smuggles in a substantive, contestable ideal of the good life — one that looks unmistakably like that of a university intellectual. A subsistence farmer in rural Guatemala, a Buddhist monk in Myanmar, or a factory worker in Shenzhen may find meaning in ritual, devotion, obedience, or quiet endurance — activities Chomsky's framework implicitly treats as insufficiently "creative" or "free." If the meaning of life is whatever we create through autonomous action, then Chomsky is not describing a universal human condition but prescribing a particular Enlightenment ideal — one that may be as culturally limited as the structures of domination he opposes.

**Strengthened Response:** Chomsky can strengthen his position by distinguishing between *first-order* and *second-order* creativity. First-order creativity is the capacity all humans exercise by virtue of having a language faculty — producing and understanding new sentences. This is universal and undeniable. Second-order creativity — choosing a life project, engaging in political resistance — is one *expression* of that capacity, not its only form. By anchoring meaning in the biological universality of linguistic creativity rather than in a specific cultural ideal of autonomous self-realization, Chomsky can respect the farmer, the monk, and the factory worker as equally human without ranking their ways of life. The normative demand then becomes: any social arrangement that systematically stifles the exercise of *first-order* creativity — for example, through drudgery that leaves no room for thought, speech, or questioning — is objectively dehumanizing. This shifts the argument from a contestable cultural ideal to a universal standard grounded in the very faculty that makes us human, while staying neutral about the particular content of any person's life project.

**Two Specific Improvements for Greater Rigor:**

1. **Add a procedural test for identifying illegitimate domination.** Rather than appealing intuitively to "structures of coercion," Chomsky could adopt a modified version of the *Principle of Universalizability*: a practice is illegitimate if its universalization would destroy the conditions for the creative aspect of language use itself. A society of masters and slaves fails because the slave's silence is not chosen — it is enforced, which denies the possibility of the very linguistic creativity that makes moral reasoning possible. This gives a formal criterion, not just an anarchist preference.

2. **Acknowledge and absorb the existentialist critique more fully.** The Contrarian section concedes that the Nazi's meaning is "not equally justified" but never fully answers the meta-ethical challenge (the question of what moral claims themselves mean): *why* is it not equally justified if there are no transcendent values? Chomsky can answer by noting that meaning is not subjective preference but *intersubjective justification* (justification that happens between people in shared reasoning) — a position already latent in his appeal to "fundamental moral equality." Making this explicit would transform his final reflection from a personal declaration into a philosophically rigorous conclusion: the meaning of life is not chosen but *recognized* in the act of taking another's creative capacity seriously.

## Reading Guide

If you want to explore Noam Chomsky's ideas further, start with his shorter political works rather than his technical linguistics — try *Understanding Power* (a collection of accessible interviews) or *Manufacturing Consent* (written with Edward S. Herman) for his analysis of media and power. For his philosophical views on human nature and creativity, *Problems of Knowledge and Freedom* offers a good entry point. Keep in mind that Chomsky writes with a dense, academic style and often assumes familiarity with the political history he references; don't hesitate to pause and look up names or events you don't recognize — the arguments are worth following closely.
