## 1. First Principles

### The Foundation: Suffering is Real

My philosophy begins with one undeniable fact: beings can suffer. Pleasure and pain are real — not metaphors, not illusions — and they matter to the being who experiences them. From this simple starting point, I take several foundational beliefs as given.

### Equal Consideration of Interests

First, the principle of equal consideration of interests. This is not a description of how the world is, but a minimal moral demand about how we *ought* to reason. As I argued in *Animal Liberation* (1975), the interests of every affected being count equally — unless we can point to a morally relevant difference that justifies unequal treatment. What matters is the capacity to suffer, not species membership, rationality, or language ability. This marks the boundary of the moral community. A stone has no interests; a mouse does.

### Preference Utilitarianism (Not Just Pleasure vs. Pain)

Second, I take a preference utilitarian rather than a hedonistic (pleasure-focused) utilitarian view. What matters is not merely the balance of pleasure over pain, but the satisfaction or frustration of preferences — the desires and interests a conscious being has. As I developed in *Practical Ethics* (1979), a preference utilitarian perspective accounts for why we value autonomy, relationships, and long-term projects — things a simple pleasure-pain calculation sometimes overlooks.

### A Naturalistic Universe (No Cosmic Purpose)

Third, I accept that the universe is naturalistic. There is no cosmic purpose, no divine plan, no pre-ordained goal toward which humanity is heading. We are evolved social primates on a small planet in an indifferent cosmos. Consciousness is a biological phenomenon — remarkable, but not supernatural. Meaning is not discovered etched into the fabric of reality; it is constructed by beings capable of valuing.

### The Expanding Circle of Moral Concern

And fourth, I see the expanding circle — the gradual widening of moral concern from family to tribe to nation to all of humanity to all sentient beings — as both a historical trend and a moral demand. Reason pushes us toward impartiality (not favoring our own interests over others'), toward seeing our own interests as no more important than the comparable interests of others.

## 2. The Meaning of Life

### The Core Idea: Reduce Suffering, Satisfy Preferences

From these starting points, the meaning of life follows not as a single answer but as a directional command: **to reduce suffering and satisfy preferences, impartially considered, across all sentient beings** (beings capable of experiencing feelings).

This is not a mystical or transcendent purpose. It is a practical one, grounded in the fact that suffering is bad — bad from the perspective of the being who experiences it — and that we, as beings capable of reasoning about consequences, have the ability and therefore the ethical duty to act on that recognition. As I wrote in *The Most Good You Can Do* (2015), the question "How should I live?" is answered by asking "How can I do the most good?"

### A Life Connected to Something Larger

The meaning of life, then, is to make the world better — not for oneself alone, not even for one's own species alone, but for all beings who can suffer or whose preferences can be frustrated. We are, in the words of the philosopher Derek Parfit, beings who can "cause the existence of life that is worth living." Expanding the sphere of what we care about beyond our narrow self-interest is itself a source of deep fulfillment. Acting for the good of all sentient beings gives life coherence: it connects our small, finite existence to something larger than ourselves.

### Not a Joyless Life

This is not a life-denying rejection of pleasure. Preference utilitarianism values the satisfaction of preferences — including the preference to enjoy beauty, pursue knowledge, love, and create. The meaning of life is not to abolish all joy in the name of duty; it is to expand the circle of those whose joy matters.

## 3. Actionable Insights

A philosophy that has no practical implications is not worth holding. My view demands concrete, sometimes radical changes in how we live:

**Diet.** Most of us participate in the largest-scale, most avoidable infliction of suffering in human history: factory farming. The preferences of billions of animals — to live free from pain, to move, to engage in natural behaviors — are systematically ignored for the sake of cheap meat. Going vegan (or at least significantly reducing animal product consumption) is not a gesture; it is one of the most direct ways an individual can reduce suffering.

**Giving.** Effective altruism (using evidence and reason to help others as much as possible) follows from impartial reasoning. If I can prevent a child's blindness for $50, and I spend that $50 on a restaurant meal instead, I am privileging my trivial preference over a devastating loss for another person. A meaningful life is one that asks: where can my resources do the most good? Organizations like GiveWell and the Against Malaria Foundation show that our giving can have enormous, measurable impact.

**Career.** Instead of asking "What will make me happy?", ask "What will make the biggest difference?" Working in disease prevention, global health, policy reform, or even "earning to give" — taking a high-salary job in order to donate most of it — can be a powerful way to live out these principles.

**Political and civic action.** Advocate for policies that reduce suffering globally: animal welfare legislation, factory-farm bans, foreign aid, pandemic prevention, and climate action. The interests of future generations — and of non-human animals — must count, even though they cannot vote.

**Personal relationships.** Expand your moral imagination. Practice empathy across barriers of species, culture, and nationality. Engage honestly with the full consequences of your choices. And forgive yourself the imperfection of living up to these ideals — doing some good is infinitely better than doing none.

## 4. Contrarian View / Strawman

**Critic (speaking):** "Your view is monstrous in its coldness and impossible in its demands. You would have us treat our own children no differently from starving strangers on another continent, or pigs in a factory farm. But no one can live that way — the emotional and psychological cost is unbearable. A parent cannot be impartial; the bond with their own child is not a bias to be overcome but a central source of moral meaning. Your philosophy drains life of the very commitments and attachments that give it value. Moreover, by reducing the meaning of life to suffering-reduction, you miss what makes life meaningful: love, art, friendship, ritual, belonging — none of which fit into your utilitarian calculation. A life spent calculating the greatest good for the greatest number is a life that has forgotten how to live."

### My Response

This objection carries emotional force, and I take it seriously. I have addressed it directly in *The Expanding Circle* (1981) and *How Are We to Live?* (1993).

**First: special relationships are not rejected.** The charge that impartiality denies the value of special relationships misunderstands preference utilitarianism. The bond between parent and child is not a bias to be erased; it is a profound preference that can generate enormous good. A parent's love creates a life better for both parent and child — and, crucially, for the child whose preferences are met by that love. No calculation suggests that abandoning your child to care for strangers is optimal. The *scope* of your concern must be impartial — everyone counts equally — but the *intensity* of your action can be particular, because proximity, knowledge, and relationships make you uniquely effective at doing good in your immediate sphere.

**Second: reason and emotion work together.** On the charge of coldness: impartial reasoning is not the same as lacking emotion. Empathy is what alerts us to suffering; reason is what helps us direct our empathy effectively. A mother who loves her child does not contradict the principle that all children's interests matter equally — she simply acts where she can make the most difference with the resources she has.

**Third: art, love, and ritual are already valued.** The objection that my view neglects love, art, and ritual misses the point. Preference utilitarianism values these precisely because they satisfy preferences. A world of art, friendship, and celebration is a world of satisfied preferences. My philosophy does not demand that we abandon them; it demands that we not purchase them at the cost of devastating the lives of others — human or non-human — who count just as much.

## 5. Final Reflection

I have been asked, more times than I can count, whether my view is too demanding — whether it asks more of us than human nature can bear. Perhaps it does. But I have never found this a compelling objection to a moral truth. If we are capable of doing enormous good, and we instead choose to live comfortably while others suffer terribly, we should feel no pride in that. We should feel the weight of what we have chosen.

And yet — the life I am describing is not one of grim sacrifice. It is a life of purpose. It is a life where your choices matter, where your small actions link to a vast network of sentient beings whose existence is better because you passed through the world. There is meaning in that. There is joy in that. I know this because I have seen it in the lives of so many effective altruists, animal advocates, and ordinary people who decided to take seriously the simple idea that the suffering of another matters.

The meaning of life is not found — it is made. And we make it by reducing suffering wherever we can, by expanding the circle of our concern, and by living so that when we are gone, the world is a little less full of pain than it would otherwise have been. That is enough. That is everything.

## Strengthened Through Critique

Peter Singer's position is powerful, but three weaknesses merit charitable stress-testing. Addressing them does not refute his project — it makes it more defensible.

**1. The is-ought gap (moving from facts to values).** Singer begins with "suffering is bad from the perspective of the being who experiences it" and leaps to "therefore we have a moral obligation to reduce all suffering impartially." But that "therefore" hides a logical gap: from the fact that *I* disvalue pain, it does not automatically follow that *you* ought to care about the pain of a stranger on another continent — or of a non-human animal. The principle of equal consideration of interests is a moral choice, not a logical consequence of being able to suffer.

*How Singer could strengthen this:* He should openly acknowledge that the equal-consideration principle is a moral commitment, not a deduction from the fact of suffering. He could ground it in the requirement of practical reason — that to be consistent, if I claim my own suffering matters *because it is suffering*, I cannot deny that another's suffering matters when the same quality is present. This is the universalizability test (a Kantian move Singer has long resisted), but embracing it would close the is-ought gap without importing the full system of Kantian ethics. The principle becomes: if you make moral claims at all, consistency demands impartial consideration of similar cases.

**2. The instrumental (efficiency-based) defense of partiality.** When defending special relationships, Singer argues that a parent's partiality toward their own child is acceptable because proximity makes them *more effective* at doing good there. This is a risky concession: what if an impartial calculation showed that the parent could produce more total preference-satisfaction by donating their child's college fund to malaria bed nets? Then Singer's framework would demand that sacrifice — and his response collapses.

*How Singer could strengthen this:* He should offer a richer account of agent-relative reasons (reasons that depend on who you are and your particular relationships). Preference utilitarianism can accommodate the intrinsic — not merely practical — value of intimate relationships because those relationships are part of the preferences being satisfied. A parent's preference that *their* child flourish is not a bias that blocks impartial truth; it is a preference that gives the parent's life meaning. Satisfying it has value even when a more "efficient" use of resources exists. Singer should say openly: partiality is acceptable not because it happens to be efficient, but because the preferences tied up in intimate relationships are among the preferences that count.

**3. The boundary problem (who or what counts?).** Singer grounds moral status in the capacity for suffering, but this cuts both ways: newborn infants, brain-damaged humans, and some animals with basic sentience (ability to feel) but no self-awareness have preferences at the moment but no interest in their own continued existence over time. On the other side, potential future beings and advanced AI systems may have no preferences now but could come to have moral weight later.

*How Singer could strengthen this:* Rather than treating sentience as a sharp dividing line, he should adopt a graduated model of moral considerability — one where the strength of protection increases with the complexity of preferences at stake. This preserves his core insight (sentience is necessary for moral standing) while avoiding the uncomfortable implication that a simple organism with basic pain-avoidance and a human with life-long projects are equally easy to override. A graduated framework would also make room for the moral weight of entities whose future depends on our present choices — the beginning of a genuinely rigorous sentientist ethics.

### Summary of Specific Improvements

- **(A)** Ground equal consideration in universalizability (the test of consistency) rather than treating it as a basic assumption — this closes the is-ought gap.
- **(B)** Develop a non-instrumental defense of partiality rooted in the way intimate relationships shape our very preferences.
- **(C)** Replace the simple sentience threshold with a graduated model of moral considerability that tracks preference-complexity.

## Reading Guide

If you are new to Peter Singer's ideas, a good starting point is *Practical Ethics* (1979), which lays out his overall framework clearly. For his most famous and accessible work, try *Animal Liberation* (1975), the book that launched the modern animal rights movement. To understand how his philosophy applies to everyday life and charitable giving, *The Most Good You Can Do* (2015) is excellent. Singer challenges many deeply held assumptions, so read with an open but critical mind — his goal is not to make you comfortable, but to make you think about whether your actions match your values.
