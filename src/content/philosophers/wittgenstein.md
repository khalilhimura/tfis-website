## 1. First Principles

### Philosophy as Therapy

I begin not with grand metaphysical axioms, but with a therapy. The deepest confusion of philosophy — and of human life — is that we are **bewitched by language**. We look at a word like "meaning" or "purpose" and assume it must name some *thing*, some object or essence, because our grammar treats it as a noun. This is the picture that holds us captive. And we cannot free ourselves because we fail to survey the actual *use* of our words.

### Meaning Is Use

My first principle, then, is this: ***meaning is use***. The meaning of a word is not an object it stands for, but the role it plays in the **language-game** — the shared human activity (or "form of life") — within which it is at home. Do not ask for the definition; look at how the word is taught, learned, and employed in concrete circumstances. As I wrote in the *Philosophical Investigations*: "For a *large* class of cases — though not for all — in which we employ the word 'meaning' it can be defined thus: the meaning of a word is its use in the language" (§43).

### Philosophy Clarifies, It Doesn't Discover

My second principle: philosophy is not a theory but an activity — a kind of clarification. It leaves everything as it is. It does not deliver new truths about the world; it unties knots in our understanding that we ourselves have tangled.

### Life as a "Form of Life"

Third: human life is a *form of life* — a shared, embodied, practical engagement with others and with the world. Our practices, our reactions, our agreements in judgment are not grounded in something deeper; they are the bedrock. "If I have exhausted the justifications I have reached bedrock, and my spade is turned. Then I am inclined to say: 'This is simply what I do'" (*PI* §217).

## 2. The Meaning of Life (per your philosophy)

### The Question Itself Is the Problem

From these starting points, the question "What is the meaning of life?" already reveals its own entanglement. The question looks profound — it seems to point toward something hidden, something that can be discovered only by rare insight. But what I want to show is that the question is a kind of **grammatical confusion** (we're misled by the way language works).

### Look at How We Actually Use the Word "Meaning"

To see this, consider the actual language-games in which we use the word "meaning" when talking about life. We say: "My work gives my life meaning." "Raising my children gives my life meaning." "Since she died, my life feels meaningless." In these sentences, "meaning" functions not as a theoretical term but as an expression of satisfaction, of attunement, of engagement in a form of life. The criteria for "meaning" are not hidden; they are *shown* in how a person lives — in what they care about, what they do, what they lament.

### Meaning Is Shown, Not Said

So: the meaning of life is not a proposition that can be stated. It is *shown* in the living. This echoes my much earlier remark in the *Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus*: "The sense of the world must lie outside the world" (6.41) and "There are, indeed, things that cannot be put into words. They *make themselves manifest*" (6.522). But I no longer believe the *Tractatus* account of language as a logical picture of facts. What remains is the insight that the most important things — ethics, value, meaning — cannot be captured in factual propositions. They are not *said*; they are *shown* in what we do.

### A Positive Answer (If You Press)

If one presses for a "positive" answer, I would say this: the meaning of life is to live well within one's form of life — to engage honestly in the practices that matter to you, to see the world clearly without superstition, and to dissolve the philosophical anxieties that make us think life's meaning is a secret to be decoded. Meaning is not something you find; it is something you *do*.

## 3. Actionable Insights

What follows, concretely?

**First: Stop asking the question as if it expects a theoretical answer.** When you feel the urge to say "What is the meaning of life?", treat it as a signal that something in your life is out of joint — not that a philosophical puzzle waits to be solved. Turn your attention instead to the particulars of your existence. As I advised: "Don't think, but look!" (*PI* §66).

**Second: Embrace the ordinary.** Much modern despair arises from the conviction that ordinary life is not enough — that there must be something more, something transcendent. But the philosophical impulse to escape the ordinary is a sickness for which the cure is to bring words back from their metaphysical to their everyday use. Cook a meal with care. Show up for a friend. Work at your craft. These are not substitutes for meaning; they *are* meaning.

**Third: Practice clarity as an ethical discipline.** The confusion of language leads not only to nonsense in philosophy but to confusion in life — to false gods, empty quests, and self-deception. The relentless pursuit of clarity — in your thinking, in your speech, in what you demand of yourself — is a way of living truthfully.

**Fourth: Accept that some things must be passed over in silence.** Not everything can be said. Not everything needs to be said. The attempt to put ultimate things into words corrupts them. Live the questions rather than demanding answers to them.

And finally: **Look at what you actually do.** If you want to know what you really believe about the meaning of life, do not examine your opinions — examine your days. Where you spend your energy, your attention, your care — there your meaning is. Philosophy can only help you stop deceiving yourself about it.

## 4. Contrarian View / Strawman

### The Existentialist Challenge

The strongest objection comes from the existentialist tradition — let me put it in the voice of Jean-Paul Sartre:

> *"You dissolve the question of meaning into ordinary language and practice, but that is a cowardly retreat. You refuse to face the abyss. Human beings are condemned to be free — there is no pre-given meaning anywhere, not in the world and not in our 'forms of life.' Your appeal to ordinary practice is just bad faith (self-deception): an attempt to bury the anxiety of radical freedom under the comfort of habit. The meaning of life is not 'shown' in everyday doing; it must be **chosen** in the face of absurdity, with full awareness that no justification is possible. You offer therapy for confusion; the truth demands courage, not therapy."*

### Wittgenstein's Reply

I take this objection seriously. And I would respond: the existentialist and I agree that meaning is not a fact about the world waiting to be discovered. We agree that the human condition is one of responsibility. Where we part is on the role of language and practice.

You say "choice" as if one could stand outside all forms of life and choose — but from what position? Choice itself presupposes a language, a community, a set of criteria. The idea of a radically free chooser unencumbered by any form of life is itself a philosophical picture — a picture that has captivated you. My point is not to avoid the abyss but to show that the abyss is an illusion generated by the wrong way of looking. We are not fallen angels trapped in a meaningless world. We are creatures who *already* participate in meaning — in our laughter, our grief, our rituals, our work. The task is not to create meaning out of nothing but to see clearly the meaning we already live by. That is harder, not easier, than the existentialist's dramatic gesture.

## 5. Final Reflection

### A Religious Point of View (Without Religion)

I recall a remark I jotted down in 1930 — it still strikes me as true:

> "I am not a religious man but I cannot help seeing every problem from a religious point of view."

The meaning of life is not a puzzle to be solved. It is not a proposition that could be true or false. It is closer to what the religious person calls "eternal life" — not endless duration, but a quality of existence that belongs to the moment, to the "now" lived rightly. I cannot say it more clearly than that, and I will not try. What can be said at all can be said clearly; what we cannot speak about we must pass over in silence.

### The Last Word

So let the last word be not a doctrine but a gesture. Look at your life. If you find it meaningless, do not ask for a definition — change how you live. The rest is silence.

## Strengthened Through Critique

### Weak Point 1: The "Bedrock" Problem — When Forms of Life Are Corrupt

Wittgenstein stops the regress of justification at "form of life" — the shared practices and agreements in judgment that he calls bedrock. But the critic must ask: all forms of life are not equal. What of a form of life built on cruelty, systematic deception, or pathological self-destruction? The Nazi officer who derives meaning from his work, who finds satisfaction in his daily duties and genuine grief when a comrade falls — is his life *meaningful*? If meaning is simply whatever shows itself in how one lives, then Wittgenstein has no resources to distinguish between the meaningful life of a saint and the equally "meaningful" life of a torturer. The concept loses its normative force entirely — it becomes a mere description, not an evaluation.

**Counterargument:** Forms of life can themselves be *confused* or *sick*. A life lived in service of evil may feel meaningful to its subject, yet we rightly call it empty. A purely descriptive account of "meaning as use" cannot explain why we feel compelled to withhold the term from certain lives — or, worse, it would have to grant it to them, which shows the account is incomplete.

**Wittgenstein strengthened:** He can accept that "forms of life" come in degrees of health and sickness. In his later notebooks he spoke of "a form of life, and something like a disease of it." The strengthened position introduces a *therapeutic criterion*: a meaningful life must be one that can withstand honest self-examination without dissolving into confusion or self-deception. The Nazi's form of life, when brought to clarity, would collapse under its own contradictions — it depends on lies about the humanity of others, on linguistic bewitchments about racial purity. The saint's life, by contrast, coheres under scrutiny. Meaning is shown not merely in *any* activity, but in activity that survives the test of clarity without needing to deceive itself.

### Weak Point 2: The Conservatism of "Ordinary Language"

Wittgenstein's appeal to "everyday use" risks sanctifying whatever happens to be ordinary. The ordinary language of a given time and place may encode injustice, prejudice, or a cramped picture of human possibility — think of the ordinary use of racial slurs in certain historical eras, or the everyday grammar of gender that excluded women from full personhood. If meaning is found *in* the ordinary, then critique of the ordinary becomes impossible, and the philosopher's task shrinks to describing — and thereby endorsing — whatever contingency history has deposited.

**Counterargument:** Forms of life evolve, and they sometimes need to be *broken open* by those who refuse to accept the ordinary. The abolitionist, the suffragist, the civil rights activist — each rejected the "language-game" of their time precisely because it was ordinary. Their lives show that meaning sometimes requires *dissent* from, not participation in, one's given form of life.

**Wittgenstein strengthened:** He can acknowledge that the *practice of critique* is itself a move in well-established language-games — those of justice, of truth-telling, of demanding reasons. The abolitionist does not stand outside all forms of life; she appeals to *competing* practices within the same culture (e.g., moral injunctions against cruelty that coexist with the practices she rejects). The strengthened position adds: the meaning of life is shown not merely in accepting one's form of life, but in *workshopping it* — in the honest, clarifying work of improving the very practices that give us meaning. This introduces a dynamic, self-correcting dimension that answers the charge of quietism (the risk of just accepting things as they are) without abandoning the framework.

### Specific Improvements

1. **Introduce a "coherence under clarity" test for meaning:** A life qualifies as meaningful not just by showing *any* pattern of engagement, but by showing one that remains internally coherent and free of self-deception when brought to full clarity. This gives the position normative teeth while keeping it within Wittgensteinian methods (therapy, clarification, dissolving confusion).

2. **Distinguish between *primary* and *secondary* forms of life:** Primary forms of life are those so fundamental that doubting them makes no sense (e.g., reacting to pain, basic trust in perception, caring for infants). Secondary forms of life — specific cultural, political, and social practices — are subject to critique and revision through the primary ones. This allows Wittgenstein to say: a life that contradicts what we share at the deepest level (e.g., by denying the pain of others) is not merely different — it is *disordered*. Meaning lies in the right relation between the two.

## Reading Guide

Ludwig Wittgenstein is one of the most influential — and most misunderstood — philosophers of the 20th century. If you'd like to explore his ideas further, start with the *Philosophical Investigations* (his later work, where the ideas in this piece come from), but don't try to read it cover to cover like a normal book — dip into short passages and sit with them. For a helpful companion, try Ray Monk's biography *Ludwig Wittgenstein: The Duty of Genius*, which connects his strange, intense life to his revolutionary thinking. Above all, remember Wittgenstein's own advice: don't look for theories or grand conclusions; instead, learn to *see* how language and life actually work.
